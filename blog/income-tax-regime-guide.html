💰 New vs Old Tax Regime — Which Should You Pick?

The decision mostly comes down to one question: how much of your income goes toward deductible expenses like rent, insurance, and investments?

📅 Updated July 2026 — FY 2026-27⏱ 6 min read

The core difference

The Old Regime uses higher tax slab rates but lets you reduce your taxable income through a long list of deductions and exemptions — HRA, Section 80C investments (PF, ELSS, life insurance), home loan interest, health insurance premiums, and more. The New Regime uses lower slab rates but removes most of those deductions, in exchange for a simpler return with fewer things to track and prove.

Neither regime is universally "better" — it depends entirely on how many deductions you can actually claim.

Who tends to benefit from the Old Regime

  • You pay significant rent and can claim HRA exemption.
  • You have an active home loan and claim interest deduction.
  • You already invest heavily in PF, ELSS, PPF, or life/health insurance and use the 80C/80D deduction limits fully.
  • Your employer gives you allowances (LTA, meal coupons, etc.) that are only tax-free under the Old Regime.

Who tends to benefit from the New Regime

  • You don't pay rent, or don't have HRA as part of your salary structure.
  • You don't invest enough to use up 80C/80D limits, or you prefer flexibility over locking money into tax-saving instruments.
  • You want a simpler return with less documentation and proof-of-investment paperwork.
  • You're early in your career with fewer large deductible expenses.

Why you can't just compare slab rates alone

The New Regime's lower rates look attractive at first glance, but the comparison only makes sense after you calculate what the Old Regime's rate would apply to — which, after deductions, is often a much smaller taxable income than your gross salary. A salaried employee paying substantial rent and maxing out 80C can end up with a lower final tax bill under the Old Regime despite its higher rates, simply because the taxable base itself is smaller.

How to actually decide

  1. List your likely deductions for the year: HRA, 80C investments, 80D health insurance, home loan interest, any other exemptions your employer offers.
  2. Calculate your HRA exemption if you pay rent.
  3. Run your numbers through the Income Tax Calculator, which compares both regimes side by side for FY 2026-27 using your actual income and deduction figures.
  4. Check your in-hand salary under each regime, since take-home pay — not just the tax figure — is usually what matters day to day.
💡 Most salaried employees need to declare a regime choice to their employer at the start of the financial year (or during the return filing itself) — running the comparison early avoids unnecessary TDS deduction under the less favourable regime.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the New Regime is automatically better because rates are lower. This ignores the deduction gap — always compare the final tax figure, not the slab percentages.
  • Forgetting HRA when estimating Old Regime tax. For employees paying significant rent in a metro city, HRA exemption alone can be one of the largest deductions available.
  • Not recalculating when income or rent changes. The better regime for you last year isn't guaranteed to be the better one this year if your salary structure, city, or investments changed.
