🏠 How Loan EMI Is Actually Calculated

Your EMI amount stays the same every month — but what it's made of changes completely from the first payment to the last.

📅 Updated July 2026⏱ 6 min read

The EMI formula

Every standard EMI (Equated Monthly Installment) is calculated with one formula:

EMI = P × r × (1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1)

where P is the loan principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and n is the total number of monthly installments (loan tenure in months). This formula guarantees the same payment amount every month for the life of the loan — but the split between interest and principal inside that fixed payment changes every single month.

Why your early EMIs are mostly interest

Interest for any given month is calculated on the outstanding principal — the amount you still owe. In month 1, the outstanding principal is the entire loan amount, so the interest portion of that month's EMI is at its highest. As each EMI gradually chips away at the principal, the interest portion shrinks slightly every month while the principal portion grows — but the total EMI amount itself never changes.

In practice, for a typical 20-year home loan, well over half of your payments in the first several years go toward interest, not toward reducing what you owe. This is normal amortization behavior, not a sign of an unfair loan — but it's exactly why loan tenure and prepayment timing matter so much.

Why a prepayment early in the loan saves more

Because interest is calculated on outstanding principal, reducing that principal earlier means every subsequent month's interest is calculated on a smaller base — the saving compounds for the rest of the loan term. The exact same prepayment amount made in year 1 saves meaningfully more total interest than making it in year 15, simply because it has more remaining months left to "work."

ScenarioEffect
Prepay a lump sum early in the loanLarger total interest savings, since it reduces the principal that interest compounds on for longer
Prepay the same amount late in the loanSmaller total interest savings — most of the "interest-heavy" period has already passed
Increase EMI amount instead of a lump sumShortens tenure faster than a one-time prepayment of similar total value, spread over time

Tenure vs interest rate — which matters more?

A longer tenure lowers your monthly EMI but increases total interest paid substantially, since you're paying interest on a high outstanding balance for more months. A small difference in interest rate, meanwhile, compounds across the entire loan — which is why comparing the effective rate between lenders (not just the advertised headline rate) before committing to a long-tenure loan is worth the extra few minutes.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing only the EMI amount between two loan offers. A lower EMI with a longer tenure can cost significantly more in total interest than a higher EMI with a shorter tenure.
  • Assuming a late-stage prepayment saves as much as an early one. As shown above, timing changes the savings substantially, even for the identical prepayment amount.
  • Ignoring processing fees and prepayment charges. Some loans charge a fee for prepayment — factor this in before assuming a prepayment is purely beneficial.
