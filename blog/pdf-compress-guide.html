📄 How to Compress a PDF Without Losing Quality

What actually makes a PDF heavy, which compression level to pick, and when to aim for an exact file size instead.

📅 Updated July 2026⏱ 6 min read🔒 100% browser-based tools used

Why PDFs get so large in the first place

Most people assume a PDF is large because it has "too many pages." In practice, page count rarely matters — images are almost always the real cause. A single page scanned at 300 DPI in full color can weigh 2-4 MB on its own; a 20-page scanned document can easily cross 50 MB. Text and vector content (fonts, lines, form fields) barely add any weight by comparison — a 100-page pure-text PDF is often smaller than a 3-page scanned one.

The second common cause is embedded fonts. Some PDF exporters embed an entire font family even if only a few characters are used on the page. And third, PDFs produced by older scanners or "print to PDF" drivers sometimes store images completely uncompressed, which is the single biggest easy win when you compress them properly.

What PDF compression actually does

A PDF compressor does two things under the hood: it re-encodes embedded images at a lower bitrate (similar to saving a JPEG at 60% quality instead of 100%), and it strips or subsets unused font data and redundant metadata. It does not delete pages, does not touch the text layer, and does not change page count or layout — only the underlying image/font data is made more efficient.

This is why compression quality settings exist as a trade-off dial, not a magic "make it smaller" switch: push the dial too far and text stays sharp (text is vector, not an image) but scanned pages or photos inside the PDF start showing visible JPEG-style artifacts.

When to use target-size compression instead of a quality slider

Most online forms — government portals, exam applications, job sites — specify an exact upper limit like "PDF must be under 200 KB." A quality slider makes you guess and re-try repeatedly. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Compressing an already-compressed PDF repeatedly. Each re-compression pass re-encodes the images again, compounding quality loss (the same way re-saving a JPEG multiple times degrades it). Always compress from the original, not from a previously compressed copy.
  • Ignoring scanned pages. If a PDF is a scanned document, the "image" being compressed is literally the entire page — be more conservative with compression level than you would for a PDF with a handful of embedded photos.
  • Assuming higher DPI always means better. For on-screen reading or portal uploads, 150-200 DPI is normally indistinguishable from 300 DPI but is a fraction of the file size.
